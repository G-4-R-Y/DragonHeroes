# Research track: pix-marketplace-legal

## Summary
PAYMENTS (Track 1): For a Pix in/out P2P marketplace with split fees, the practical shortlist as of mid-2026 is Asaas, Mercado Pago, OpenPix/Woovi, and Efí. Asaas offers white-label subaccounts created via API with delegated KYC (ID document + selfie + mandatory declared income, ~48h review, plus a mandatory "regulatory evaluation period" for new API subaccount operations), native split, Pix acquiring up to 0.99%, and Pix transfer (payout) APIs — the best white-label fit for paying out individual (CPF) sellers. Mercado Pago's Split de Pagamentos requires every seller to hold a Mercado Pago account linked via OAuth (Split 1:1 is self-serve; 1:N requires commercial negotiation); KYC is fully absorbed by MP but the UX forces sellers off-platform. OpenPix/Woovi does Pix-only splits via subaccounts keyed to the seller's Pix key (CPF/CNPJ) with commission retained automatically — lightest integration, but thinner KYC means the studio retains more compliance burden. Efí (ex-Gerencianet, a licensed payment institution) has the strongest raw "envio Pix" (Pix send) API for high-volume payouts to any Pix key, plus subaccounts. PagBank has an Orders API with split but is oriented to conventional e-commerce. Stripe is a poor fit: Pix for Brazil-based businesses remains invite-only in 2026 and Connect+Pix depends on merchant-of-record constraints (Stripe's 2026 Pix expansion, partly via EBANX, targets US merchants selling into Brazil). EBANX has a mature Pix payout API (/ws/payout/create with Pix-key ownership verification) but is an enterprise cross-border processor — only relevant if the studio bills from a foreign entity. Structurally, the studio should keep all funds in the PSP's escrow/subaccount layer and never intermediate payments itself, to stay outside BCB payment-institution licensing.

LEGAL STATUS (Track 2) — this is where the model faces existential risk, and the landscape hardened dramatically in 2025-2026. Lei 15.211/2025 (ECA Digital, in force 17 March 2026) defines "caixas de recompensa" (paid random virtual-item mechanisms) and prohibits them in any game directed at, or likely to be accessed by, under-18s, with fines up to 10% of Brazilian gross revenue, suspension of the game in Brazil, and reported minimum fines of R$50M for serious violations; ANPD is the enforcement authority and is phasing in supervision of reliable age verification (self-declared age is no longer acceptable — CPF/biometric-grade verification required; phase 2 supervision of game/app providers from August 2026). In June 2026 the 1ª Vara da Infância do DF issued Brazil's first loot-box condemnations: ~R$298-333M in collective moral damages across Riot Games (R$15M), Sony, Microsoft, Valve, Nintendo, Google, Apple and others, holding loot boxes to be abusive embedded advertising and a defective service under the CDC, "structurally comparable to gambling," and ordering probability disclosure, randomness warnings, high-reliability age verification, refunds to minors, R$100k/day fines (on appeal). Critically for Dragon Heroes: cash-out DOES worsen classification. Lei 14.790/2023 regulates "jogos online" (RNG-determined outcomes wagered for money) under an SPA/MF license costing R$30M for 5 years with GGR tax at 12%, escalating via LC 224 to 13% (2026), 14% (2027), 15% (2028); operating gambling without a license remains the art. 50 LCP contravention plus exposure to the illegal-bets enforcement wave (PF Operation Véu de Maia, July 2026). The New York AG's February 2026 suit against Valve is the template regulators cite: paid randomness + a market converting items to real money = illegal gambling. Dragon Heroes' saving distinction is that items drop from gameplay rather than from paid chance — the model is defensible ONLY if no paid RNG (keys, chests, gacha, paid rerolls) exists anywhere in a game whose items are cashable. Skin/item wagering itself remains an acknowledged regulatory gap in Brazil, which cuts both ways: no license path exists, and enforcement uses general gambling/consumer law.

TAX (Track 3): The studio's fee is a paid intermediation service — today subject to ISS (2-5%, municipal), PIS/COFINS, and IRPJ/CSLL (or Simples Nacional), with an NFS-e issued for each commission. 2026 is the tax-reform test year: CBS 0.9% + IBS 0.1% must be highlighted on invoices (optional on NFS-e initially) and are creditable against PIS/COFINS; CBS fully replaces PIS/COFINS in 2027 and ISS phases out through 2032. LC 214/2025 (arts. 22-23) is the sleeper issue: "digital platforms" that intermediate transactions and control payment/terms/delivery become jointly liable for IBS/CBS of national suppliers who fail to issue fiscal documents, and substitutes for foreign suppliers; the platform must register as a contributor, report all intermediated operations, and — when it initiates payment — provide the data for tax split payment at financial settlement (a safe harbor applies if reporting and split duties are met). Player-sellers (individuals) owe 15% capital-gains tax (GCAP) on profit, but sales of "bens de pequeno valor" up to R$35,000/month are exempt — covering virtually all casual players; habitual for-profit trading risks recharacterization as ordinary income (carnê-leão, up to 27.5%) or a demand to formalize as a business. The platform has no IR-withholding duty on P2P sales today (the recent RFB withholding rules on platform commissions apply to licensed betting operators), but Pix flows are fully visible to Receita via PSP reporting, so the studio should issue annual transaction statements to sellers.

AML/KYC & LGPD (Track 4) and PRECEDENTS (Track 5): Game items and NFTs were expressly left OUTSIDE the new virtual-asset (VASP) regime — Resolutions BCB 519/520/521 (Nov 2025, in force Feb 2026, authorization filings due by Oct 2026) follow Lei 14.478/2022's definition, which excludes items granting access to specific products/services — so the marketplace does not need BCB authorization as an SPSAV. It is also not squarely an "obligated person" under Lei 9.613/1998, but RMT marketplaces are classic money-laundering vectors (value can be moved by selling a rare item cheap to an accomplice), the regulated PSP will impose its own PLD/FT requirements on the flows, and the July 2026 PF operation shows aggressive enforcement against betting-adjacent laundering — so a voluntary COAF-aligned PLD program (seller KYC with CPF validation, payout only to a Pix key owned by the verified account holder, velocity/price-deviation monitoring, 5-year records) is strongly advised. LGPD applies fully (payment data, CPF, and — if ECA Digital age checks use biometrics — sensitive data requiring a DPIA); minors' data processing must follow best-interest rules, reinforcing an 18+ gate. Precedent-wise, Steam's Community Market survives precisely because it is closed-loop (wallet funds cannot be cashed out) and Valve bans third-party RMT — the NY suit attacks the leakage to cash-out sites; Brazil's June 2026 condemnations show courts will act under CDC/ECA without waiting for gambling regulators, and no Brazilian first-party cash-out marketplace precedent exists to shelter behind.

## Recommendations
- Make Dragon Heroes 18+ with ECA Digital-grade age verification (CPF/document/biometric, not self-declaration) as a launch blocker: a real-money marketplace plus randomly dropped items in a game accessible to minors combines Lei 15.211/2025 fines (up to 10% of Brazil revenue) with the exact fact pattern behind the June 2026 R$300M+ TJDFT condemnations.
- Never sell paid randomness: no purchasable loot boxes, keys, chests, gacha, or paid rerolls anywhere in the game, since items are cashable. Keep all random drops earned exclusively through gameplay and sell only direct-purchase items — this is the single strongest defense against classification as an unlicensed 'jogo online' under Lei 14.790/2023 (whose license costs R$30M/5yr plus 13-15% GGR tax) or the art. 50 LCP gambling contravention.
- Obtain a formal legal opinion (parecer) from a Brazilian gaming/betting law firm specifically on the cash-out design before launch, and consider a phased rollout: closed-loop wallet first (Steam model — proceeds spendable in-game only), adding Pix cash-out later once the opinion, age-gating, and AML controls are proven.
- Adopt the TJDFT ruling's compliance package proactively even at 18+: publish exact drop-probability tables, display randomness warnings on relevant screens, and provide a refund channel — these are now the de facto CDC standard for random mechanics in Brazil.
- PSP selection: shortlist Asaas (white-label subaccounts with delegated KYC, native split, Pix out) as primary, with OpenPix/Woovi (lean Pix-key subaccount splits) or Efí (cheapest high-volume 'envio Pix' payouts) as alternatives; use Mercado Pago Split only if forcing sellers to open MP accounts is acceptable; do not plan on Stripe (Pix invite-only for Brazilian entities in 2026); consider EBANX only if settling from a foreign entity. Contractually confirm the chosen PSP supports marketplace/escrow flows for P2P goods and ask about their LC 214 tax-split-payment roadmap.
- Keep the studio out of the money flow: all buyer funds must sit in PSP escrow/subaccounts and payouts must run on PSP rails, so the studio never becomes an unlicensed payment institution under BCB rules; take the fee as an automatic split to the studio's account.
- Tax structure: issue an NFS-e for every commission charged; join the 2026 CBS/IBS test regime (0.9%+0.1% highlighted, creditable); register the platform as an IBS/CBS contributor and build the LC 214/2025 art. 22-23 reporting and split-payment data capabilities now, since platform joint liability becomes real as rates ramp from 2027.
- Player tax support: provide sellers an annual statement of sales and gains, surface an in-product notice that gains are taxable at 15% via GCAP with the R$35,000/month small-value exemption, and require CPF at seller onboarding so statements and any future reporting duties are feasible.
- Build a voluntary COAF-aligned PLD/FT program: verified-CPF seller KYC, payouts only to Pix keys owned by the verified holder (use PSP key-ownership verification APIs), transaction monitoring for wash-trading and off-market pricing (laundering via deliberate underpriced item sales), volume limits, and 5-year record retention.
- LGPD: appoint a DPO, run a DPIA (RIPD) covering payment data and biometric/document age verification, minimize retention of verification artifacts, and document legal bases (contract execution for marketplace data, legal obligation for KYC/tax records).
- Structure weekly tournaments as skill-predominant competitions with sponsored prize pools; avoid designs where entry fees fund prizes determined by chance, which would look like fixed-odds betting requiring SPA authorization.
- Watch the classificação indicativa: the Ministry of Justice's 2026 guide includes the descriptor 'Compras on-line (inclui itens aleatórios ou apostas)' — expect the marketplace to drive an 18 rating, which should be embraced, not fought.

## Caveats
- The June 2026 TJDFT loot-box condemnations (Riot R$15M; ~R$298-333M aggregate across Sony, Microsoft, Valve, Nintendo, Google, Apple et al.) are first-instance and under appeal; the legal standard could soften or harden on appeal, and reported aggregate figures vary between sources (R$298M vs R$333M).
- No Brazilian statute, SPA rule, or court decision yet squarely addresses a FIRST-PARTY cash-out marketplace for gameplay-earned items; the analysis that gameplay-earned drops avoid gambling classification is a reasoned inference from Lei 14.790's paid-wager element, not settled law — a regulator could still argue the game time/monetized ecosystem constitutes consideration, as the NY AG did against Valve.
- ECA Digital (Lei 15.211/2025) regulation is still being issued: ANPD published only preliminary age-verification guidance in March 2026, with sector supervision expanding in August 2026 — specific technical requirements for games may change; the reported R$50M minimum fine for serious violations comes from secondary press coverage and should be confirmed against the statute's final text.
- LC 214/2025 platform-liability and tax-split-payment provisions await infralegal regulation; how they apply to C2C sales between non-contribuinte individuals (most Dragon Heroes sellers) is not yet fully defined, and 2026 is only the test year — obligations firm up from 2027.
- PSP commercial terms, KYC flows, and product availability (Asaas subaccount rules, Mercado Pago 1:N access, Stripe's invite-only Pix status, OpenPix subaccount features) change frequently; several details come from vendor docs/blogs and need confirmation in commercial negotiation, including whether each PSP's risk policy accepts a virtual-item RMT marketplace at all (some PSPs classify it as high-risk/gambling-adjacent and refuse).
- Betting-sector tax escalation (LC 224: GGR 13% in 2026 rising to 15% in 2028) and possible future regulation of skin/item wagering — flagged by experts as an open gap — could bring virtual-item marketplaces into SPA scope by new rulemaking with little notice.
- The Lexology analysis of the loot-box ruling returned HTTP 403 and was corroborated via TJDFT's own press release and Conjur instead; income-tax treatment of habitual virtual-item sellers rests on general capital-gains and recharacterization doctrine, as Receita Federal has issued no ruling specific to game items.
- This is research synthesis, not legal advice; a licensed Brazilian attorney must validate the gambling-classification, ECA Digital, and tax positions before launch, especially given daily-fine and revenue-percentage penalty exposure.
